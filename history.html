Chapter one

The History of HBCUs

Understanding the past helps explain the importance of these institutions today.

The beginning

Education in the face of barriers

Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs) are institutions of higher education established primarily to educate Black Americans during a time when many colleges and universities excluded them.

Before and after the Civil War, Black Americans faced major barriers to formal education. HBCUs helped create opportunities for students to pursue education, develop professional skills, and prepare for leadership roles in their communities.

The United States Department of Education defines an HBCU as an institution established before 1964 whose principal mission was, and is, the education of Black Americans.

Important milestones

A timeline of HBCU history

Explore several important moments in the development of HBCUs.

1837

Cheyney University is established

Richard Humphreys provided funds to establish an institution dedicated to educating Black students. It is recognized as the nation's oldest HBCU.

1865

The Civil War ends

The end of the Civil War and the abolition of slavery created new opportunities and increased demand for education among formerly enslaved people and their families.

1881

Tuskegee Institute is founded

Tuskegee was established in Alabama. Booker T. Washington became its first teacher and founding principal.

1887

Florida A&M University begins

FAMU began as the State Normal College for Colored Students in Tallahassee, Florida, with 15 students and two instructors.

1904

Mary McLeod Bethune opens a school

Bethune opened the Daytona Literary and Industrial Training School for Negro Girls with five students.

Today

HBCUs continue their legacy

HBCUs continue to educate students, support research, develop leaders, and contribute to communities across the United States.
